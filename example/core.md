# The idea

Hessling grinders are handed down. A kitchen grinder goes from parent to child; an industrial grinder gets rebuilt instead of replaced. The brand behaves the same way: it says only what will still be true later, and it keeps its promises longer than anyone checks.

# In practice

- For consumers: the grinder is shown being used, worn and repaired, not only new ([Workshop light](glossary/imagery/workshop-light.md)).
- For roasteries: specifications, spare parts and service life come before adjectives ([Trade voice](glossary/voice/trade-voice.md), [Exact over warm](glossary/principles/exact-over-warm.md)).
- For everyone: no claim we could not repeat in thirty years ([Food-contact and durability claims](glossary/claims/food-contact-and-durability.md)).
- In behaviour: a quote, an order or a warranty claim is never decided against the customer by default ([When no one answers](glossary/behaviour/when-no-one-answers.md)).

# When no rule covers the case

1. Ask what will still be true and useful in thirty years. Start there.
2. Give the exact fact first: the number, the date, the part.
3. If a choice affects a customer's money, a claim, or the product, hand it to the owner of that area. See [Who decides](glossary/principles/who-decides.md).

# What it is not

Not nostalgic. "Passed on" is about durability and service, not about looking old. The product is current; it is built to last.

# Why

Hessling cannot compete on price with grinders made to be replaced. It competes on the fact that it is still there, with parts, when the others are gone.
